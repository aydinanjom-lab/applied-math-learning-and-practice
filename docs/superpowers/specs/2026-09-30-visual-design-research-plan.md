# Fix Napkin's numerals, thumb reach and icon first

Napkin's palette is not what is holding it back; three cheaper things are. First, the app's `font-variant-numeric: tabular-nums` does nothing, because the Lexend build served by Google Fonts has no tabular-figure feature at all (verified by inspecting the font file), so the timer, streak and stats digits jitter and misalign; routing digits through the system font or Inter fixes it in under an hour. Second, every primary action (Reveal, Check, Next, Start) sits wherever the content ends rather than in the bottom-center band that thumbs reach most easily, and the Yes/No self-grade pair appears in a different place from the Reveal button it replaces; a fixed bottom action bar with 56 px full-width buttons and a 12 px gap between Yes and No is the highest-value layout change. Third, the current icon breaks four documented platform rules at once: it contains text ("25%"), it pre-rounds its corners, it serves one file as both "any" and "maskable", and the tab favicon uses an off-palette blue (#1f4fd6) instead of the fixed indigo; a folded-corner square mark on the indigo tile satisfies Apple, Android and the web-manifest safe zone with two flat shapes. Beyond those three, the evidence supports modest changes: drop body line-height from 1.5 to 1.4 for a 40-character column, raise the revealed answer to 40–44 px at line-height 1.1, move Start above the drill-set and mode choices on the home screen, make the progress bar segmented, remove card shadows in dark mode in favor of borders, and gate all motion behind `prefers-reduced-motion`. The result screen's order (verdict, answer, explanation, method, star notice, Next) already matches what Duolingo, Brilliant, Khan Academy and the feedback literature recommend and should stay. Total effort for the whole plan is roughly 22 hours, of which the top-priority items are about 8.

## Lexend's reading-speed claim is thin, but the real problem is its missing tabular digits

The case for keeping Lexend on legibility grounds is neutral; the case for changing how digits are set is strong. Lexend's "empirically shown to improve reading" line rests on one study of **20 third graders reading aloud for one minute** against Times New Roman at 16 pt, run by the font's creators and never independently replicated at scale ([ReadingQuick](https://readingquick.com/articles/lexend-font-review.html); [Bold by Text Control](https://bold.textcontrol.com/article/2019/8/14/a/lexend-a-font-is-changing-the-way-the-world-reads/)). The only large peer-reviewed comparison, Wallace et al. 2022 with **352 adults and 16 fonts**, found reading speed differs by up to 35% between an individual's best and worst font but that **no font is fastest for everyone**; ordinary faces such as Garamond, Montserrat and Noto Sans did best on average, and Lexend was not tested ([Adobe Research](https://research.adobe.com/publication/towards-individuated-reading-experiences-different-fonts-increase-reading-speed-for-different-individuals)). The closest precedent, OpenDyslexic, has repeatedly failed to beat Arial or Times in controlled studies ([BYU Editing Research](https://editingresearch.byu.edu/2020/05/28/which-fonts-are-best-for-dyslexia/)). The practical upshot is that a 19-year-old reading a ten-word prompt should expect no measurable gain from Lexend over Inter or SF, and no loss either. Direct measurement of the two Google Fonts builds shows they are near twins at the same pixel size (both have an x-height to cap-height ratio of 0.75; Lexend runs about 2.5% wider), so switching would not change the app's feel much ([google/fonts Lexend](https://github.com/google/fonts/tree/main/ofl/lexend); [google/fonts Inter](https://github.com/google/fonts/tree/main/ofl/inter)).

What does matter is numerals. The same font inspection found Lexend's OpenType feature list contains **no `tnum`, `pnum`, `lnum` or `onum`**, and its digits range from 500 to 620 units wide, so the widest digit is 24% wider than the narrowest and `tabular-nums` has nothing to switch to ([google/fonts Lexend](https://github.com/google/fonts/tree/main/ofl/lexend)). Napkin's `.num` class, applied to the timer ("3.4s"), the streak, and stats, therefore has no effect, and any live counter will shift as digits change. Tabular figures exist precisely to "prevent layout shifts of dynamically-changing content, such as a timer counting down" ([Piccalilli](https://piccalil.li/blog/control-changing-numbers-with-font-variant-numeric)), and Google's Wear OS type system carves out a separate Numeral role with tabular spacing "to prevent character jumping during scrolling/animation" ([Android Wear OS type tokens](https://developer.android.com/design/ui/wear/guides/styles/typography/type-scale-tokens)). Inter's build ships `tnum`, `pnum`, `cpsp` and an optical-size axis ([google/fonts Inter](https://github.com/google/fonts/tree/main/ofl/inter)); SF Pro on iOS also honors `tabular-nums`. The cheapest fix is to give `.num` a system-font stack (zero download) or Inter, keep Lexend for prose, and leave the revealed answer in Lexend since a static number does not need tabular spacing.

The scale itself needs only trimming. Apple's Body style is 17 pt and Material's body-large is 16/24 px, so the app's 17 px body is right ([Kodeco Dynamic Type table](https://www.kodeco.com/books/auto-layout-by-tutorials/v1.0/chapters/11-dynamic-type); [material-web type tokens](https://github.com/material-components/material-web/blob/main/tokens/versions/v0_192/_md-sys-typescale.scss)). But a 358 px column at 17 px Lexend holds only about **38 characters per line**, already under Bringhurst's 45–75 and Butterick's 45–90 ([UXPin on line length](https://www.uxpin.com/studio/blog/optimal-line-length-for-readability/); [Practical Typography](https://practicaltypography.com/summary-of-key-rules.html)), and short lines drift apart at generous leading; the body should sit at the low end of Butterick's 120–145% range, 1.4 rather than the current 1.5. The 26 px prompt lands between Apple Title 2 (22) and Title 1 (28) and is fine. The revealed answer should be the largest, heaviest thing on screen: Material's display-small is 36/44 and Duolingo sets display at 56–64 px weight 700–800 with about −0.01 to −0.02 em tracking ([shadcn.io Duolingo design](https://www.shadcn.io/design/duolingo)); 40–44 px at weight 700, line-height 1.1 and the existing −0.02 em still fits "$1,234,567" in the column. Section labels currently run at 15 px uppercase; the evidence on all-caps slowing reading by roughly 14% is about sentences, not two-word labels ([Perea et al. on Tinker](https://www.uv.es/mperea/lowercaseUPPERCASE_reading.pdf)), and Material's own label styles use positive tracking at 11–14 px, so 12–13 px, weight 600, 0.06–0.1 em tracking and CSS `text-transform` (so screen readers get sentence case) is the right shape ([LogRocket on casing](https://blog.logrocket.com/ux-design/casing-letters-enhances-visual-hierarchy)). Nothing below weight 400 should appear anywhere in light mode.

## Strong signifiers are already there; put them where the thumb is and make the press geometry honest

Napkin's primary button already has the Duolingo-style solid bottom shadow, which is the right instinct. The evidence behind that pattern is not "3D beats flat" but "strong signifiers beat weak ones": NN/g's eyetracking study of **71 users across 9 page pairs** found people took **22% longer and made 25% more fixations** on pages with weak (flat) signifiers ([Aaron Gustafson's summary of NN/g](https://www.aaron-gustafson.com/notebook/links/flat-ui-less-attention-cause-uncertainty/)). No controlled study shows the bottom-shadow button itself improves learning or engagement; it is a brand signature Duolingo borrowed from casual games ([webdesignhot Duolingo design.md](https://www.webdesignhot.com/design.md/duolingo/)). What the current CSS gets slightly wrong is the geometry: the primary travels 3 px while its shadow drops from 4 px to 1 px (consistent), but the Yes/No buttons travel 2 px while their 3 px shadow vanishes, so their bottom edge visibly moves. The pressed illusion depends on translate distance equaling shadow height, and on a transition short enough to feel instant, around 50–80 ms rather than the current 100 ms ([GeeksforGeeks pressed effect](https://geeksforgeeks.org/how-to-add-a-pressed-effect-on-button-click-in-css)). Apple's HIG is blunt that a custom button without a press state "can feel unresponsive" ([Apple HIG, Buttons](https://developer.apple.com/design/human-interface-guidelines/buttons)), so the secondary buttons' 1 px nudge should stay.

Placement matters more than shape. Hoober's observation of **1,333 people** found 49% hold the phone one-handed and thumbs drive about 75% of interactions, with two-thirds of one-handed grips right-handed, which is fewer than the roughly 90% of people who are right-handed ([A List Apart, How We Hold Our Gadgets](https://alistapart.com/article/how-we-hold-our-gadgets/); [UXmatters](https://www.uxmatters.com/mt/archives/2013/02/how-do-users-really-hold-mobile-devices.php)). The resulting thumb-zone map puts the easy band at bottom-center and the hard band at the top corners, so primary calls to action belong bottom-center ([Smashing Magazine, The Thumb Zone](https://smashingmagazine.com/2016/09/the-thumb-zone-designing-for-mobile-users)). In Napkin today, Reveal sits directly under the prompt near the top of the screen; after Reveal, the Yes/No pair renders below the answer and explanation, further down; on the result screen, Next follows the method and star notice. The thumb travels a different distance on every step. A fixed bottom action bar (safe-area padded, `100dvh` aware) holding the single primary at each step, with the Yes/No pair occupying the same band Reveal just vacated, removes that travel; Duolingo's Check and Continue share a spot for exactly this reason ([Pratt IXD critique](https://ixd.prattsi.org/2021/09/design-critique-duolingo-web-app/)). Anki's maintainers warn that merging Show Answer with the grade buttons "can cause an accidental double tap" ([Anki forums](https://forums.ankiweb.net/t/simple-but-very-useful-change-show-answer-with-number-buttons/18045)), so the bar should ignore pointer events for about 200 ms while the Yes/No pair fades in. Sizes are settled: WCAG 2.2 sets a 24 px floor, Apple 44 pt, Material 48 dp with 8 dp between targets ([W3C Understanding SC 2.5.8](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG22/Understanding/target-size-minimum.html); [Google accessibility help](https://support.google.com/accessibility/android/answer/7101858?hl=en)). Napkin's 48 px minimum passes; 56 px on the two or three primary buttons costs nothing, and the Yes/No gap should widen from 8 to 12–16 px because the two actions are opposites and a mis-tap corrupts the star log. Since the palette cannot carry more meaning, the pair should differ by icon and label as well as color, the way Kahoot's answer buttons differ by shape ([Tech & Learning on Kahoot](https://techlearning.com/how-to/kahoot-lesson-plan-for-elementary-grades)); the app already does this with "✓ Yes, I had it" and "✗ No, missed it".

The feedback content is right and should not be touched. Hattie and Timperley's synthesis of **196 studies** puts feedback's average effect at 0.70–0.79 against 0.40 for interventions generally ([Hattie & Timperley PDF](https://www.uky.edu/~gmswan3/575/Hattie_Timperly_2007.pdf)); Kulik and Kulik found immediate feedback beats delayed in applied quiz settings ([Springer abstract](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/BF02319855)); and Metcalfe's review shows errors followed by corrective feedback plus explanation are learned best, with high-confidence errors the most correctable ([Metcalfe, Learning from Errors](https://wesleyan.edu/ofcd/resources/OFCD%20Resource%20Docs/Learning%20from%20Errors.pdf)). Napkin already shows the exact answer, the explanation and, on a miss, the method, then stars automatically. The typed-input path is also mostly right: `type="text" inputmode="decimal"` is the recommended combination and the 24 px field avoids iOS's focus zoom, which triggers below 16 px ([specification.website mobile inputs](https://specification.website/spec/accessibility/mobile-form-inputs/)); adding `enterkeyhint="done"` and `autocorrect="off" spellcheck="false"` finishes it. One compliance gap remains: the Y, N, Enter and Space shortcuts are single printable keys with no way to turn them off, which fails WCAG 2.1.4 for speech-input users unless they are scoped to a focused component or made switchable ([W3C Understanding 2.1.4](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG21/Understanding/character-key-shortcuts)); a localStorage "keyboard shortcuts" toggle on the About screen resolves it.

## The current icon breaks four platform rules; a folded-corner square fixes all of them

Napkin's `icon.svg` is a tilted paper card carrying the text "25%" on an indigo tile with `rx="112"` corners, exported to 192 and 512 px PNGs, with the 512 declared as `"purpose": "any maskable"` and the apple-touch-icon pointing at the 192. The favicon is a separate inline SVG with a "%" glyph on `#1f4fd6`, a blue that is not in the fixed palette. Each of these collides with a documented rule. Apple's icon guidance asks for "a single, centered point," prefers text "only when it's an essential part of your experience or brand" because it "is often too small to read easily," and warns that detail "can make an icon appear muddy, especially at smaller sizes" ([Apple HIG, App icons](https://developer.apple.com/design/human-interface-guidelines/app-icons)). Touch icons "are expected to be full bleed, with square corners" and pre-rounded corners "render strangely and/or incorrectly under some circumstances" ([Joplin Discourse quoting Apple](https://discourse.joplinapp.org/t/discourse-apple-touch-icon-is-formatted-incorrectly/14241)), and the recommended apple-touch-icon is an opaque 180 px file ([RealFaviconGenerator](https://realfavicongenerator.net/blog/apple-touch-icon-is-180x180-pixels-but-is-that-still-true)). Android's adaptive icons put the visible area in the inner **66 dp of a 108 dp** canvas and require clean edges with no baked-in masks ([Android Developers, Adaptive icons](https://developer.android.com/develop/ui/views/launch/icon_design_adaptive)); the web maskable rule is stricter, keeping essential content inside a circle of **40% of the width** because the outer 10% may be cropped ([web.dev, Maskable icons](https://web.dev/maskable-icon)). The three "25%" glyphs on the current card push toward that edge and thin out below 48 px, where icon detail "tends to blur or disappear" ([Uxcel iconography](https://app.uxcel.com/courses/design-systems-intro/iconography-standards-997/balancing-simplicity-and-detail-for-legibility-4975)). Apple's iOS 26 tinted and clear modes add a fourth test: the mark must read as a single-color silhouette ([Create with Swift on Icon Composer](https://www.createwithswift.com/crafting-liquid-glass-app-icons-with-icon-composer/)).

The examples that work share one owned color, one silhouette-legible subject, and one idea. Duolingo is "built around a single owned color" and one character ([Canny Creative](https://www.canny-creative.com/brand-breakdown/brand/duolingo/)); Things is a flat tile plus one cobalt checkmark ([shadcn.io Things](https://www.shadcn.io/design/things/raw)); Notion fuses a letter with a physical object so it stays one subject ([brandkit.pro Notion](https://brandkit.pro/brand/notion)). Anki is the cautionary case: its icon was meant to be a card, but "the star has become the more recognizable part" because it carried two ideas ([Anki forums](https://forums.ankiweb.net/t/ankis-star-logo/5203)). For Napkin the recommended mark is a paper-colored square of side 256 on the 512 canvas (corners at 0.386 W, inside the 40% circle), rotated −8°, with its top-right corner cut along a 72 px diagonal and a deep-indigo triangle sitting in the cut as the fold's underside: two flat shapes, one accent, no text, silhouette-safe. The favicon uses the same shapes in near-black on transparent with an embedded `prefers-color-scheme: dark` style swapping to paper, a technique that follows the OS setting rather than the browser theme ([favicon.im](https://favicon.im/blog/favicon-for-light-dark-modes)). The file set becomes `favicon.svg`, a 32 px `favicon.ico`, an opaque 180 px `apple-touch-icon.png`, `icon-192.png` and `icon-512.png` as `"any"`, and a separate `icon-maskable-512.png` with the napkin group scaled by 0.9, all exported from one master SVG with `rsvg-convert` or ImageMagick.

The wordmark needs less. Lowercase suits a common noun about informal arithmetic, and Butterick's rule is that lowercase "doesn't ordinarily need letterspacing" while all-caps always gets 5–12% ([Practical Typography, Letterspacing](https://practicaltypography.com/letterspacing.html)); Linear, the closest product analogue, sets Inter at display sizes with roughly −2.2% tracking and prefers the wordmark over the bare mark "as space allows" because it "has stronger brand recognition" ([stfo.io Linear audit](https://www.stfo.io/research/audits/linear/); [Linear brand guidelines](https://linear.app/docs/brand-guidelines)). Napkin's h1 already uses 700 weight and −0.01 em; the change is to set it lowercase, pair it with the mark at cap height in the home header, and let a single accent (the fold flap, or the i-dot in indigo) tie the two together. Keep it as rendered text, not outlined paths, so it stays accessible and needs no design software.

## Start belongs above the choices; the result and summary screens mostly follow the evidence already

The home screen is the one place where Napkin's order runs against the evidence. Today the user scrolls past the streak line, an optional Sunday-check card, the Interview-runs card, six drill-set rows, a mode pair and a count triple before reaching Start, which then sits above six text links. Apple's HIG says to "keep the number of prominent buttons to one or two per view" because too many "increases cognitive load," and to "place most important items near the top" ([Apple HIG, Buttons](https://developer.apple.com/design/human-interface-guidelines/buttons); [Apple HIG, Layout](https://developer.apple.com/design/human-interface-guidelines/layout)). Hick's law puts decision time proportional to the log of the number of options ([Laws of UX](https://lawsofux.com/hicks-law/)), and Duolingo's 2022 redesign replaced a grid of choices with a single path precisely because learners "weren't sure whether they were using the app the 'correct' or 'best' way" ([Duolingo blog, new home screen](https://blog.duolingo.com/new-duolingo-home-screen-design)). Since Napkin remembers the last set, mode and count, Start can move directly under the status line with its label carrying the remembered choice ("Start · Quick math · 10 · Say it") and the starred count, with the choice controls below it for anyone who wants to change them. The Sunday check is the only competing prominent button and can stay, since it appears one day a week. Hierarchy should follow Apple's "use style, not size" rule: only Start is filled; drill-set rows stay as outlined cards; the six links become a compact row.

The result screen needs no reordering. Duolingo's bottom panel shows the verdict, the meaning, an "Explain my answer" link and Continue ([Lazyweb Duolingo flow](https://www.lazyweb.com/canvas/flows/duolingo/start-lesson)); Khan Academy tells you immediately and offers the step-by-step behind a balloon ([Khan Academy support](https://support.khanacademy.org/hc/fr/community/posts/360002144911/comments/360000561852)); Brilliant "encourages learners to read the explanation even after a correct answer" ([Brilliant, how it teaches math](https://brilliant.org/resources/choosing-brilliant/how-brilliant-teaches-math/)). Napkin's verdict pill, answer, explanation, method, star notice and Next match that sequence, and the explanation is shown by default rather than hidden, which is right for a ten-minute-a-day user. The change is spatial: the verdict should tint the fixed bottom panel rather than sit as a pill in the flow, and the method box, which appears only on a miss, is the one element worth keeping visible on correct answers too, per Brilliant's argument. Spacing conventions are consistent across both platforms: Material's card corner is 12 dp with 1 dp outline and 0–1 dp elevation, with 8 dp between cards on mobile ([Material Components Card.md](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/material-components/material-components-android/master/docs/components/Card.md); [Material Shape.md](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/material-components/material-components-android/master/docs/theming/Shape.md)), and an 8 pt grid scales to whole pixels at every device multiplier ([LS Graphics, 8pt grid](https://www.ls.graphics/blog/why-8pt-grid-the-math-behind-clean-ui)). Napkin's 16 px radius, 16 px gutter and 8/16/24/40 spacing tokens already sit inside those ranges; the only adjustment is one card style per screen (the home screen currently mixes shadowed cards, a filled locked card and a star-tinted notice card, which reads as a false hierarchy).

Progress and summary are where small changes pay. The one empirical progress-indicator study found, across **2,230 quiz respondents**, that a continuous linear bar performed best on completion while users say they prefer partitioned displays ([Chalmers thesis](https://odr.chalmers.se/items/cbaa69d0-53c1-432f-9e9f-9c1b221a6c19/full)); with only 5, 10 or 20 items per session, a segmented bar gives both, plus a place to mark starred items, as long as segments fill in constant steps (fast-then-slow bars are perceived as longer, per [arXiv 2211.13909](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2211.13909)). The session summary should follow Duolingo's three-tile shape and Elevate's one-word rank ([Elevate support, ranks](https://support.elevateapp.com/hc/en-us/articles/4402971705499-What-does-a-Good-Great-and-Excellent-rank-mean)), and the goal-gradient literature says to show the count-down to a near goal rather than lifetime totals: Kivetz et al. found people accelerate as visible progress nears a goal, and an endowed head start of two stamps cut completion time by **about 20%** ([Columbia Business School abstract](https://business.columbia.edu/faculty/research/goal-gradient-hypothesis-resurrected-purchase-acceleration-illusionary-goal)). "2 starred items left to clear" and "day 5 of 7" are that count-down for Napkin. Stats should be a 3-tile strip over a table with right-aligned tabular numbers, 48 px rows and hairline dividers, with an inline SVG sparkline per drill set once three sessions exist; the number gives the magnitude and the sparkline the shape ([Visualizing.org, sparkline](https://www.visualizing.org/sparkline)). Empty states for Starred and Stats should say what the screen is for and offer the first action, which NN/g identifies as the purpose of empty states ([NN/g, empty states](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/empty-state-interface-design/)).

## Motion should happen once per event, and dark mode should lean on borders, not shadows

Apple's motion guidance is that "brief, precise feedback animations feel lightweight and unobtrusive," that designers should "avoid motion on frequent UI interactions," and that motion "should NOT be the only way to communicate important information" ([Apple HIG, Motion](https://developer.apple.com/design/human-interface-guidelines/motion)). Stasko's peripheral-display study found animated displays distract from the main task, with start-and-stop motion the least distracting ([Stasko, Interact 2001](https://faculty.cc.gatech.edu/~john.stasko/papers/interact01.pdf)). The duration numbers converge: about 100 ms for a state change, 200–300 ms for a panel, 500 ms as the ceiling ([Val Head](https://valhead.com/?p=2978); [Uxcel on NN/g durations](https://app.uxcel.com/courses/design-foundations/motion-995/duration-9224)), with Material's easing tokens giving decelerate for entering and accelerate for exiting elements ([Flutter Material Easing](https://api.flutter.dev/flutter/material/Easing-class.html)). Napkin sees each of these transitions twenty times a session, so the budget is small and each item fires once: the bottom panel fades in over 200 ms, a check mark draws over 300 ms on a correct answer only, the progress segment fills over 150–200 ms, the three summary tiles count up once over about 600 ms, and a streak tick happens only on the day's first completed session. WCAG 2.3.3 asks that interaction-triggered motion be disableable, and `prefers-reduced-motion` is the sufficient technique ([W3C Understanding 2.3.3](https://w3c.github.io/wcag/understanding/animation-from-interactions.html)); Napkin's stylesheet already gates button and progress transitions, and the new panel and count-up need the same gate, with the reduced fallback being the final state and an instant tint.

Dark mode in Napkin already avoids the worst mistakes: the canvas is #0e1016 rather than pure black, text is #eceef4 rather than #ffffff, and accents are lifted in lightness. Material's dark baseline is dark grey "which increases visibility for shadows and also reduces eye strain for light text," but it also concedes that "shadows are less effective in an app using a dark theme" and now expresses elevation through lighter surface-container tones rather than shadow ([Material Dark.md](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/material-components/material-components-android/master/docs/theming/Dark.md); [Material 3 in Compose](https://developer.android.com/develop/ui/compose/designsystems/material3)); Apple uses dimmer base and brighter elevated background sets for the same purpose ([Apple HIG, Dark Mode](https://developer.apple.com/design/human-interface-guidelines/dark-mode)). Napkin currently does the opposite, replacing the light-mode shadow with a stronger black one in dark mode. With the palette fixed, the available levers are to drop card shadows in dark mode, rely on the existing 1 px `--border` and the `--surface`/`--surface-2` step, and keep one shadow for the floating bottom panel. Type is the other lever: light text on dark "bloats" through halation ([Adobe Design](https://adobe.design/ideas/keeping-type-consistent-in-changing-conditions)), and the accepted fix for a variable font without a GRAD axis is to reduce weight by about 50 in dark mode, 700 to 650 on the answer and 400 to 370 on body ([Adam Argyle](https://nerdy.dev/adjust-perceived-typepace-weight-for-dark-mode-without-layout-shift)). Lexend has a weight axis but no GRAD axis (verified by font inspection), and the current `@import` requests only four static instances, so the stylesheet must request the range `wght@300..700` for intermediate weights to render. Timer digits keep a fixed weight so they do not jitter.

## Sourcing note

The research sandbox's proxy blocked direct reads of many primary pages across all four topics, including nngroup.com, m3.material.io and m2.material.io, developer.apple.com body content for several pages, w3.org, MDN, practicaltypography.com, web.dev, blog.duolingo.com, and the Kivetz, Chalmers and Shaver-Troup papers. Where a finding in this report cites one of those pages, it rests on a search-engine excerpt that quotes or paraphrases the page, or on a secondary source that does; the substance is reliable but exact wording should be re-checked before quoting it verbatim. Four things were verified directly and carry more weight: the Material 3 type-scale, card, shape and dark-theme token values (read from Google's GitHub sources), the OpenType feature sets and digit widths of the Lexend and Inter Google Fonts builds (downloaded and inspected with fontTools), Android's adaptive-icon page, and the Apple HIG pages on Buttons, Layout, Lists and tables, Color, Dark Mode and Motion (fetched as JSON). Third-party reverse-engineered Duolingo CSS values disagree with each other on details (2 px vs 4 px press travel, shadow vs border) and none are Duolingo's own documentation. No controlled study was found on 3D versus flat filled buttons, on side-by-side versus stacked Yes/No pairs, on the optimal size of a single revealed number, or on what a session summary should show; those items in the plan are triangulated from platform guidance, not measured.

## Conclusion

The pattern across all four areas is that Napkin's designer chose well on the things that are hard to change later (palette, feedback content, screen order, spacing tokens) and left gaps on the things that are easy to test for and easy to miss: a CSS feature the font cannot honor, a button that moves between steps, an icon with text in it, a shadow inverted for dark mode. None of the fixes needs a designer or a new dependency; the whole plan below is CSS, a few lines of JS, and one SVG. The research also sets a ceiling on expectations: no font change will move reading speed, no animation will improve learning, and no icon concept has been user-tested. What the evidence does promise is fewer mis-taps, digits that hold still, an icon that survives every mask, and a home screen where the next step is obvious, which is the same thing Duolingo found it needed in 2022.

## Deliberately not recommended

Switching the whole app from Lexend to Inter or the system font is not recommended as a readability fix: the only strong study found no universal winner and Lexend was not tested, so the switch would be taste, not evidence; the tabular-digit problem is solved by scoping one class. Confetti, mascots, per-item celebrations and hover-lift on cards are out: no learning evidence supports them, Apple and Stasko say frequent motion distracts, and the app is seen twenty times a session. Sound is not recommended as a default; the user studies out loud in short sessions in shared spaces, so if it is added it stays off by default with two short distinct cues. Reducing dark-mode text to 87% opacity, as Material does, is unnecessary here because the fixed dark text (#eceef4) is already off-white, and changing its rendered value would edge past the fixed-palette rule. A "25%" or handwritten-fraction icon is rejected because text in icons fails Apple's guidance and dissolves below 48 px; it can live in onboarding copy. Zebra-striped stats rows are an Apple macOS multicolumn recommendation, not an iOS one. An in-app light/dark toggle that fights the OS setting is discouraged by Apple; if one exists it should default to "system". Positive letterspacing on the lowercase wordmark breaks Butterick's rule. Stacking Yes over No full-width is rejected in favor of the side-by-side pair, because vertical stacking forces thumb travel and raises the chance of hitting the wrong opposite action, though this is reasoned from target-size guidance rather than a direct study. Finally, the result-screen order and the feedback content should not be reworked; they already match the strongest evidence in the notes.
